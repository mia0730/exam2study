Exam 1 Review

Everything from the first exam is still here, in full. Exam 2 is not cumulative as far as the syllabus goes — but several ideas carry straight across, so the connections below are worth ten minutes even if nothing else here is.

Spend your time here first

If you're short on time tonight, read only the five bridges below. They're the places where Exam 1 material directly explains Exam 2 material — which means they're the places where a question could reasonably touch both.

The five bridges

1. Blow counts — borings → piles

On Exam 1, the Standard Penetration Test drove a split spoon with a 140 lb weight falling 30 inches, and N = blows for the last 12 inches. That N told you the soil's relative density or consistency, and let you estimate its bearing capacity.

On Exam 2, that estimate is what the geotechnical engineer uses to calculate a pile's design strength — the slides say so directly. And the pile driving log records blows per foot and blows per inch the same way a boring log records blow counts. Both answer "how hard was it to push steel into the ground."

2. Bearing capacity — soil strength → footing size

Exam 1 defined bearing capacity and showed how it's estimated from N. Exam 2 turns it into arithmetic: Footing Area = Load ÷ Bearing Capacity. The whole reason site exploration happens is so somebody can put a number in that denominator.

3. Vibration works on granular soil — rollers → pile hammers

Exam 1: vibratory roller for granular soils (vibration rearranges loose grains), sheepsfoot roller for cohesive soils (clay needs kneading). Exam 2: vibratory pile hammers are best suited to granular soils, where vibration liquefies the soil around the pile. Same physics, different machine.

4. Bottom-up concrete placement — tremie → augered piles → drop tubes

Exam 1: the tremie pipe places slurry wall concrete from the bottom up, keeping its discharge end buried, to prevent segregation and cement washout. Exam 2: an augered pile fills from the bottom up as the auger withdraws, and a drop tube keeps concrete from free-falling more than 4 feet into a wall or column — again, to prevent segregation. One principle, three applications.
