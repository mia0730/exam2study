3.1 · Mixing concrete

Off-site methods are more common than on-site.

Off site

Ready mix

Concrete is mixed at the batch plant, placed in the truck, and delivered.

ASTM: must be discharged within 90 minutes of adding water to the mix.

Transit mix

Dry ingredients are loaded at the batch plant and the water is added en route or at the jobsite — which restarts the clock and suits long hauls.

90 minutes is the number to remember, and the reason ready mix and transit mix are different things at all. Past 90 minutes the concrete has started to set and can no longer be properly placed.

On site

  • Small portable mixers — about 2 cubic feet capacity. Since 1 cu yd = 27 cu ft, that's roughly 14 batches to make one cubic yard. Only for very small work.
  • Stationary mixers — a batch plant set up on the jobsite, 4–12 cu yd. Used on remote sites where delivery isn't practical, or on very large pours. The Deer Island treatment plant was his example.

Parts of a concrete truck

Rear discharge or front discharge. Either way: the hopper (where it's loaded), the rotating mixing drum, the water tank, and the discharge chute.

3.2 · Transporting & placing — the five rules

These apply no matter which method you use to move the concrete:

Keep the concrete agitated until discharged

To avoid early stiffening — the drum keeps turning for a reason.

Avoid methods that expose concrete to drying out

Place carefully to eliminate segregation

Do not let concrete free fall more than 4 feet

Use drop tubes or elephant trunks for tall walls & columns.

Avoid delays — place as soon as the truck arrives

3.3 · Segregation

Segregation is when the mix separates — the heavy aggregate parts company from the cement paste — resulting in non-uniformity and unsatisfactory properties in the hardened concrete.

The three causes — know all three

1. Excessive vibrationOver-vibrating shakes the heavy aggregate to the bottom and floats the paste to the top
2. Dropping from excessive heightsHence the 4 ft rule and the drop tube
3. Moving concrete horizontally a long distanceDragging it across a slab separates it — place it where it goes

3.5 · Consolidating

Why: to ensure the concrete completely fills the forms and that entrapped air is removed.

How it's done

  • Poking with a steel rod or shovel — for small, shallow jobs
  • Internal vibrators — submerged into the concrete. Hand-held vibrators are called spuds or pokers, and are the common tool
  • External vibrators — vibratory screeds (especially on flatwork, which vibrate & screed at the same time) and vibrators attached to the forms
The two defects, side by side

Too little consolidation → honeycomb. Voids in the hardened surface where you can see the aggregate, because the concrete never filled the form.
Too much consolidation → segregation. Over-vibration separates the mix.

Both are failures of the same step, in opposite directions. If a question describes a visible defect, ask which direction they overdid it.

3.6 · Building a slab on grade

Everything that happens before the concrete shows up. Nine steps:

Strip topsoil / loam

Organic material must come out — it decomposes and settles.

Prepare the subgrade

May require more excavation, or compacted fill to bring the ground up to the required grade.

Install underground utilities

If required — easier now than through a finished slab.

Install crushed stone or other permeable fill

Two jobs: promotes good drainage, and acts as structural fill to support the loads on the slab.

Install formwork

Edge forms, with the top set to the proper elevation and adequate bracing.

Install material for the joints

Isolation joint material goes in now — before the pour.

Install the moisture barrier

Plastic film under the slab, to stop ground moisture wicking up through it.

Install reinforcing

Lightly loaded slab → light gauge welded wire fabric (WWF).
Heavily loaded slab → heavy gauge WWF or rebar.

Place, consolidate, finish, cure

Covered next.

3.7 · Placing & finishing a slab on grade

Five steps — and the same steps are followed for slabs above grade. This is the single most likely sequencing question on the exam.

Place & spread the concrete

Place by chute, wheelbarrow, buggy, bucket & crane, conveyor belt or pump.

Spread with concrete rakes — also called mud rakes or come-a-longs.

Consolidate

Done to remove entrapped air. Vibration is used — hand-held vibrators are common, and vibratory screeds vibrate and screed at the same time.

Screed (also called strike-off)

Done to level the surface and bring it even with the top of the forms.

Must work the coarse aggregate down before the concrete begins to harden.

Done with an aluminum or wooden straight edge (a screed stick). Other types: vibratory screed, roller screed.

Leaves ridges and large voids on the surface — which is why step 4 exists.

Bull float

Done right after screeding, using a bull float and magnesium hand floats ("mag floats").

  • Brings up the paste
  • Fills large surface voids
  • Begins to create a smooth surface

Final finishing

Covered in detail below — this is the step with the waiting in it.

Drill: put the finishing steps in order

Final finishing — the timing is the whole thing

When do you start?

After most of the bleed water has evaporated from the surface, and when the concrete can support the weight of a worker with only a slight indentation — 1/8 inch. This may be several hours after bull floating.

Bleed water is water that rises to the surface after placement. Finishing while it's still there works that water back into the surface and ruins it.

What floating actually does

Floating begins the final finish, and does four things:

  • Drives small aggregate into the slab
  • Brings more water to the surface
  • Removes any small waves in the surface
  • Fills any small holes in the top of the slab

Note the blade distinction: float blades are wider and slip over the finishing blades, which are narrower.

Edging

  • Edges of walkways are rounded to give a finished appearance & avoid sharp edges that may chip off
  • Tooled into the slab at the time of finish floating and troweling

3.8 · Joints in concrete work

Different types for different purposes. Some allow movement between adjacent parts, some do not. Some have reinforcing running across the joint, some do not. The four most common:

Construction joint

  • To subdivide a large slab or long wall into sections that are cast on different days
  • Acts as a pour stop
  • Reinforcing usually runs across the joint to lock adjacent parts together
  • Usually constructed to prevent motion between connected parts
  • Movement is prevented by running rebar across the joint to act like dowels, or by using a product that forms a keyway along the edges

Control joint

  • Used to control random cracking that occurs when concrete shrinks as it cures
  • A groove tooled or cut part way down into a slab to give any potential crack a preferred location
  • Two ways to make it: tooled into the slab by hand at the time of finish floating & troweling, or saw cut after the concrete has cured for a day or two — while the concrete is still "green"
  • Depth: ¼ of the slab depth, minimum
  • Control joints may not work if the groove is too shallow — the crack just goes wherever it wants

Random cracking is caused by shrinkage as the concrete cures; it is unsightly and prone to chipping and widening over time. You can't stop the cracking — you can only choose where it happens.

Isolation joint

  • Isolates the slab from walls and columns
  • Helps prevent cracking caused by differential settlement
  • The slab can settle a different amount than adjacent walls and columns without cracking
  • Can use anything that prevents bonding of two adjacent concrete surfaces — examples: roofing paper, fiberboard, joint tape
  • Slab reinforcing ends at the joint
  • Around an interior column, a diamond-shaped area is boxed out and cast later; fiberboard can be left in place to create the separation

Expansion joint

  • A gap between two sections of concrete to allow for expansion when the concrete is exposed to higher temperatures
  • Usually not required indoors
  • In a sidewalk, reinforcing stops on each side of the joint; dowels are usually not placed across it
  • The gap is filled with a flexible material like fiber board or caulking

3.9 · Casting a concrete wall

The footing goes first, then the wall. Note the three things cast into the footing so the wall can connect to it.

The footing first

  • Footing cast first to support the wall; size determined by the Engineer
  • Keyway — a groove, often formed using lumber. Forms a mechanical connection between footing and wall
  • Waterstop (optional) — blocks water from passing through the joint
  • Reinforcing dowels — a structural connection to the wall that transfers stresses to the foundation

Then the wall — the full sequence

Lay out & install forms on ONE side, including ties

Then anchor & brace, and coat with form oil.

Install reinforcing

Install embeds & cutouts

If required — anything that has to be cast in.

Install bulkheads

The end stops that close off the end of the pour.

Inspect

Last chance — once the second side goes up you can't see in.

Erect the second side

Plumb & brace

Establish pour height

Place

Use a drop tube to avoid free fall; consolidate with vibration.

Finish the concrete

Strike off level with the top, smooth out with hand trowels.

Cure

Strip forms, break ties, point & patch

"Point & patch" = filling the tie holes and repairing surface defects.

3.10 · Form pressure & placement rate

Fresh concrete behaves like a liquid, so it pushes sideways on the forms. The deeper the "liquid head," the harder it pushes.

Faster pour rate= higher stresses on the formwork
Pressure against the formsincreases with height
As concrete setspressures are reduced — it stops behaving like a liquid

Tall walls are poured in lifts

A lift is one horizontal layer of the pour. Pouring in lifts lets you:

  • Take advantage of set — the lower lift stiffens and stops pushing
  • Minimize form design loads
  • Minimize buckling or blowouts in the formwork
But you can't wait too long

You cannot delay too long between lifts — unwanted cold joints result. A cold joint is where the first lift hardened prior to the placement of the second lift, leaving a visible, weak seam with no bond across it.

So lift timing is a balance: too fast and the forms blow out, too slow and you get a cold joint.

Two formwork failures to recognize by name

  • Buckled form — the form bends under the pressure
  • Blowout — formwork failure where the concrete bursts out of the form entirely

3.12 · Elevated floor systems

Preparation, before any formwork goes up

  • Prepare, submit & approve Engineered Shop Drawings for the formwork
  • Construct supporting walls & columns up to the slab

The construction sequence

Erect formwork

Falsework, decking, pans (if needed). Falsework is the scaffolding tower system; aluminum beams carry plywood decking.

Install mechanical sleeves, blockouts & electrical rough-ins

Clean the deck/pans; apply form oil

Place rebar

Order of rebar placement: beam reinforcing first, then bottom bars for the slab, then top bars for the slab.

Chairs & bolsters hold the rebar off the forms so concrete can get underneath it (that's cover).

Place, consolidate & finish the concrete

Same five finishing steps as a slab on grade.

Cure

Remove formwork; set re-shores

Often when concrete reaches 75% of design strength — may take 7 to 14 days depending on temperatures and the type of cement used.

Drill: put the elevated-slab steps in order

Re-shoring

  • Done to support construction loads on a completed floor while concrete continues to gain strength
  • Construction loads include the weight of formwork for the next level
  • Re-shoring allows for a faster cycle time to complete each floor

The logic: a floor at 75% strength is strong enough to strip, but not strong enough to carry the weight of building the next floor on top of it. Re-shores put temporary posts back under it so you can keep moving up.

Post-tensioning

In a slab & beam system, post-tensioning cables (tendons) are laid in with the rebar before the pour. After the concrete has cured, the tendons are tensioned with a hydraulic jack, leaving elongated strands projecting at the edge which are then cut off and patched. This squeezes the concrete, putting it into compression — which it is good at — so it can span further with less depth.

3.13 · Curing concrete

The sentence the whole handout is built on

Concrete hardens by curing — not by drying out.

Why moisture is essential

  • Premature loss of moisture results in incomplete hydration — the chemical reaction that causes the concrete to harden stops when there is no moisture left to react with the cement
  • Concrete cannot recover from premature drying out — the ultimate strength will be reduced, permanently
  • The problem is most severe in flatwork (slabs) — the large surface area exposed to air is prone to rapid drying, which causes excessive shrinkage and cracking at the surface

Two ways to keep the moisture in

Use a covering
to prevent evaporation of surface water after final finishing

  • Plastic film (polyethylene sheets)
  • Canvas
  • Saturated burlap
  • Moist straw
  • Roofing paper
  • Moist sand or sawdust
  • Chemical membrane — a curing compound

Provide moisture
after final finishing

  • Misting — periodically spraying the slab surface with a fine spray to replace surface moisture lost to evaporation
  • Fogging — increases the relative humidity of the air above the slab and reduces evaporation from it
  • Ponding or immersion — the contractor piles earth or sand around the slab perimeter to form a dyke; the dyke is filled with water, keeping the slab surface submerged
Misting vs. fogging — a common mix-up

Misting puts water on the slab. Fogging puts water in the air above the slab. Different targets, same goal.

Curing compound — know the details

A liquid sprayed on the slab surface after final finishing. It forms a temporary film to trap moisture and prevent evaporation. It is lightly colored to show coverage (so the applicator can see what they've missed) and it wears off in time.

The full list of curing methods from the Week 7 slide

Misting/fogging · Covering with polyethylene sheets · Covering with burlap · Applying curing compound · Ponding · Curing blankets · Heated enclosure · Covering & injecting steam

Temperature matters just as much

The ideal curing temperature for concrete is between 50 and 85 degrees F.

TemperatureWhat happens
Below 50°FSlows down the curing process — concrete will take longer to gain its ultimate strength
Slightly below freezingCan be tolerated if the concrete is covered with curing blankets to trap the heat of hydration
Well below freezingDangerous, especially right after final finishing. Water that has not reacted with the cement will freeze and not be available for hydration. Also, water expands upon freezing, creating internal pressures. Ultimate strength greatly reduced
Above 85°FRapid loss of water due to evaporation and incomplete hydration. Also heat build-up within the concrete leads to more shrinkage cracking. Ultimate strength can be greatly reduced

Hot weather strategies

Keep the temperature of the batch as cool as possible:

  • Make the concrete with chilled water or water mixed with ice
  • Shade stockpiles of aggregate from the sun; mist the aggregate to keep it cool
  • Use a retarder in the mix to reduce the heat of hydration and slow down the curing process

During and after placement:

  • Schedule the pour for a cooler time of day, or even at night
  • Shade equipment used to handle and transport the concrete
  • Avoid prolonged delays in placing and finishing
  • Take extra precautions to retain moisture after the concrete is finished

Cold weather strategies

Keep the temperature of the batch as warm as possible (but not hot):

  • Use warm water when mixing
  • Store aggregate indoors where it can be warmed to at least room temperature; aggregate stored outdoors should be free from frozen lumps or ice
  • Use a high early cement (Type III) or an accelerator to reduce the critical curing time once the concrete is placed

During and after placement:

  • For slabs on grade, do not place concrete on frozen ground
  • After final finish, cover the concrete with curing blankets
  • In severe temperatures, enclose the area and use a vented heater
Why "vented"?

An unvented heater dumps combustion gases — including carbon dioxide — into the enclosure, which reacts with the fresh surface and ruins it (carbonation), as well as being a carbon-monoxide hazard for the crew. If a question offers "heater" vs. "vented heater," the handout says vented.
