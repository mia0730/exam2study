On which type is the lead designer usually an architect? Which usually does not employ an architect?

Building projects — the architect usually heads the design team, because of their expertise in esthetics and spatial planning.

Heavy projects — the design team often does not include an architect, because functionality is of primary importance and esthetics is often secondary or of no importance. (There are some exceptions — a signature bridge, for instance.)

Why is building construction broken down into so many different specialty areas?

Because a building isn't one thing — it's a stack of many different systems, each built from different materials by different trades, each requiring its own specialized knowledge, tools, licensing and experience. Think about what's in one building: foundations, structural frame, envelope, roofing, plumbing, electrical, HVAC, fire suppression, elevators, finishes. No single contractor is expert in all of it, so the work is divided among a general contractor and many specialty subcontractors, each licensed and skilled in their own area. That specialization is what makes the quality and safety of each system achievable.

What is the role of an architect on a building construction project?

Three things, straight off the slide:

  1. Provide an overall design that meets the owner's needs, stays within the owner's budget, and is esthetically pleasing, functional and safe.
  2. Provide plans and specifications from which the building can be built.
  3. Check for compliance with those plans and specs during construction.

The architect also heads the design team, which includes engineers, landscape architects and interior designers.

Bonus: who heads the construction team, and what is their role?

The Construction Manager (CM), because of expertise in managing budgets, creating and monitoring schedules, and coordinating construction activities.

The CM's role is to ensure the project is delivered on time and within budget, and that all work is done safely and according to the plans and specs.

A general contractor and many subcontractors are employed to actually build the project. On some projects the general contractor acts as the CM.

2 · Building codes & zoning ordinances

The one-line distinction — learn this

Zoning controls WHAT you may build on a piece of land and HOW BIG it may be — the use, the density, the bulk, where it sits on the lot.
Building codes control HOW the building must be constructed so that it is safe to occupy — structure, fire resistance, egress.

What are the objectives of a zoning ordinance?
  • Serve as a key component in the implementation of a master plan to control future development within a city or town.
  • Regulate land use to control the type of development and the density of development.
  • Preserve historic neighborhoods and provide limitations on building appearance — height, style, materials, colors.

The McDonald's example from the slides makes this concrete: the prototypical red-and-yellow McDonald's versus the one in Lexington, MA, which has a peaked roof, muted colors and a cupola — because Lexington's zoning restricts appearance.

What does a zoning ordinance actually contain?

A zoning map

Divides the city or town into districts. Each district gets a designation related to the uses permitted within it. Examples from the slides:

  • R-1 Single family residences
  • R-2 Multi-family residences
  • B-1 Retail business
  • L-1 Light industrial

The Cambridge zoning map handout is a real example — C-1/C-2 residence districts, BA/BB business districts, IA/IB industry, OS open space, SD special districts, and so on.

Written regulations that spell out the details

  • Detailed definition of each type of district and the uses allowed within it
  • Overall height and/or the number of stories
  • Setback distances — minimum distances from the building sides to the lot lines (front, side, rear)
  • Lot coverage — percentage of land area covered
  • Floor area ratio — ratio of total floor area to lot area
  • Parking requirements — number of spaces, including those for the handicapped
  • Restrictions on style and appearance

Likely exam question: which three items regulate the density of development? Setback distances, lot/floor coverage, and Floor Area Ratio.

Computing Open Space Ratio and Floor Area Ratio
FAR = Total gross building area ÷ Lot area
OSR = Open space ÷ Lot area × 100
Open space = Lot area − Building footprint − Parking lot
Lot Coverage = (Footprint + Parking) ÷ Lot area × 100 = 100% − OSR

The Anytown, MA example — 27,000 sf lot, 4-story condo, 10,000 gross sf per floor, 2,000 sf asphalt parking, floors 10 ft each. Limits: max height 45 ft, max FAR 3.00, min OSR 50%.

  • Height = 4 × 10 ft = 40 ft < 45 ft ✓ OK
  • FAR = (4 × 10,000) ÷ 27,000 = 40,000 ÷ 27,000 = 1.48 < 3.00 ✓ OK
  • Open space = 27,000 − 10,000 − 2,000 = 15,000 sf
  • OSR = 15,000 ÷ 27,000 × 100 = 56% > 50% ✓ OK
  • Lot coverage = 12,000 ÷ 27,000 × 100 ≈ 44% = 100% − 56% ✓

Setbacks on that lot: front 20 ft, side 15 ft (each side), rear 25 ft — drawn in from each lot line to define the buildable area.

Small thing worth knowing

Slide 30 rounds the lot coverage to 45%; the revised solution sheet gives 44%, which is what 12,000 ÷ 27,000 actually works out to (44.4%). If you're asked to compute it, do the division and give 44%. The check that always works: OSR + Lot Coverage = 100%.

What is a variance?

An exception to the zoning regulations, granted to an owner or designer through an appeals process when strict application of the ordinance would create a hardship. Typically heard by a zoning board of appeals.

Example: your lot is unusually shaped and you can't meet the 25 ft rear setback; you apply for a variance to build at 18 ft.

How are zoning ordinances and building codes enforced?

Zoning

  • Each city or town has a zoning official responsible for enforcing and interpreting the ordinances.
  • Before building permits are issued and construction can begin, plans are reviewed for compliance. In small cities and towns this review may be done by a building inspector.
  • During or after construction, inspectors may be used to check for compliance.
  • Violations can result in fines or mandatory abatement (correction) of the non-conforming portions of a project.

Building codes

  • Enforced through the building permit process. Plans are reviewed by an individual from the Building Department (usually an inspector) for compliance with the building code. Violations must be corrected before a permit is issued.
  • Then through inspections during construction at defined milestones, and finally the Certificate of Occupancy, which is only issued once the finished building complies and may legally be occupied.
Fire safety vs. structural safety vs. life safety — telling them apart

The study guide explicitly asks you to recognize the difference. Sort by what the requirement is protecting against:

CategoryProtects againstTypical code requirements
Structural safetyThe building falling down or deflecting excessivelyDesign loads (live, dead, snow, wind, seismic); allowable stresses; foundation bearing; material strength; deflection limits
Fire safetyFire starting, spreading, or collapsing the structureFire ratings of assemblies (hours); fire-resistive construction types; fire separations & firewalls; sprinklers & standpipes; flame-spread ratings of finishes
Life safetyPeople being unable to get out, or being injured in normal useMeans of egress — number, width and travel distance to exits; exit signs & emergency lighting; stair and guardrail dimensions; occupant load; alarms & detection; accessibility

Quick test: a 2-hour rated wall = fire safety. Two exits required from this room = life safety. Floor designed for 100 psf live load = structural safety.

Note that these overlap in practice — a fire-rated stair enclosure serves both fire safety and life safety — but on a multiple-choice question, ask what the requirement is fundamentally for.

Building code problems like Lab 1 — occupancy, construction type, height & area

Lab 1 was "Understanding Building Codes," doing exercises using the International Building Code (IBC). The tables will be provided on the exam — what you need is the method. The chain of reasoning in an IBC problem is always the same:

  1. Determine the occupancy classification from what the building is used for. The IBC letter groups: A Assembly, B Business, E Educational, F Factory, H High hazard, I Institutional, M Mercantile, R Residential, S Storage, U Utility. A building with more than one use is a mixed occupancy.
  2. Determine the type of construction — Types I through V, describing what the structure is made of and how fire-resistive it is. Type I is the most fire-resistive (noncombustible, highest ratings); Type V is the least (combustible, e.g. wood frame). Each type is subdivided A and B, where A carries higher fire ratings than B.
  3. Look up the allowable height and area in the table for that occupancy + construction type combination. The table gives allowable stories, allowable height in feet, and allowable area per floor.
  4. Apply increases if permitted — most commonly a large increase for an automatic sprinkler system, and sometimes a frontage increase for open perimeter.
  5. Compare the proposed building against the allowable. If it exceeds, you must upgrade the construction type, add sprinklers, or divide the building with firewalls.
  6. Check the fire-resistance rating table for the required rating (in hours) of each element — structural frame, exterior and interior bearing walls, floor construction, roof construction — for that construction type.
  7. Check means of egress — occupant load, number of exits required, exit width, and maximum travel distance.

The relationship to remember: a more fire-resistive construction type lets you build taller and bigger. Adding sprinklers also lets you build taller and bigger. Higher-hazard occupancies are allowed less height and area.

Permits — what's required before a permit is issued, and what other permits exist?

Before a building permit is issued

  • An application is filed, along with drawings stamped by the designers (architects and engineers). The stamp is why professional registration matters.
  • The application is usually made by the general contractor on behalf of the owner.
  • Plans are reviewed by the Building Department (usually an inspector) for compliance with the building code. Violations must be corrected before the permit is issued.
  • A check for compliance with zoning regulations is usually made at this time as well.
  • The general contractor must also typically hold the appropriate license (in Massachusetts, a Construction Supervisor's License) to pull the permit.

Separate trade permits

Required for plumbing, electrical and gas services. These are secured on behalf of the owner by licensed tradespeople — the licensed plumber pulls the plumbing permit, the licensed electrician the electrical permit.

Other permits (examples from the slides)

  • Blasting
  • Demolition
  • Asbestos removal
  • Connection to water & sewer mains
  • Lane closure

These exist largely to ensure work is being done by qualified people, to safeguard the public. Each permit has a fee.

OSHA regulations cover all work environments, not just construction sites.

  • Most frequent citations — Fall Protection.
  • Falls are the leading cause of death among construction workers.

If you remember one OSHA fact for a fill-in: fall protection is the most frequently cited violation.

Who is responsible for creating and implementing a safety program on a building project?

The general contractor. As the party controlling the site, the GC creates and implements the overall site safety program — site-specific safety plan, toolbox talks, orientations, PPE enforcement, fall protection plans, and coordination of all trades.

Each subcontractor remains responsible for the safety of its own employees and for complying with the GC's program. The owner and CM set safety expectations contractually. But the question "who is responsible for creating and implementing the safety program" → the general contractor.

Why must designers be registered and contractors licensed? How does someone become licensed?

The slide's heading says it: "Other Safeguards For The Public." Licensing and registration is required of many project participants to ensure a minimum level of competency and experience — so that the public can rely on the safety of what gets designed and built.

  • Registration of architects & engineers — this is why permit drawings must be stamped. The stamp is the registered professional taking legal responsibility for the design.
  • Licensing of contractors, subcontractors and inspectors. Massachusetts examples from the slide: Construction Supervisor's License, Plumber's License, Electrician's License, ACI Concrete Inspector's License, Massachusetts Hoisting Engineer, and others.

What it takes to become licensed as a plumber or electrician

A combination of three things:

  1. Education in the form of training
  2. A minimum number of hours of on-the-job experience
  3. Passing an exam

The study guide says explicitly: "you don't need to know specifically how many hours of training or on the job experience." Just know the three ingredients.

The Massachusetts Hoisting Engineer license is graded by machine class — Class 1 Hoisting (derricks, lattice cranes, telescoping booms, forklifts), Class 2 Excavating (excavators, backhoes, front-end loaders), Class 3 Tower/Electric/Air, Class 4 Specialty (drill rigs, concrete pumps, catch basin cleaners, sign hangers, mowers).

Who hires the surveyor?

The owner hires the (licensed/registered professional land) surveyor. The survey is a piece of information about the owner's property that the design team needs in order to start designing — so it is procured by the owner and provided to the architect. The architect requires the survey, but the owner engages it.

Subsurface exploration and environmental studies — purpose and use

Subsurface exploration (covered fully in Topic 9)

Purpose: determine what's under the ground before a foundation can be designed — the kinds of soil present and the condition of each layer, the location of the groundwater table, and the depth to bedrock. Used by: the geotechnical engineer, to recommend a foundation type and allowable bearing pressure; then the structural engineer to design the foundation; and the contractor to price excavation, dewatering, shoring and rock removal.

Environmental studies

Purpose: identify contamination, wetlands, protected habitat, hazardous materials in existing structures (asbestos, lead), and any regulatory constraints on developing the site. Used to: determine whether the site can be developed at all, what remediation will cost, what permits are needed, and what liability the owner is taking on.

A brownfield is a previously developed site whose redevelopment is complicated by real or perceived contamination — old industrial land, former gas stations, rail yards.

Methods an environmental engineer may recommend to mitigate contaminated soil

The two from the vocabulary list are the two to know cold:

  • Bioremediation — using living microorganisms (bacteria, fungi) to biologically break down and consume contaminants in the soil, converting them into harmless byproducts. Can be done in place or in treatment cells on site.
  • Phytoremediation — using plants to clean the soil. The plants take up contaminants (especially heavy metals) through their roots and store or break them down; the plants are then harvested and disposed of. Slow, but inexpensive and low-impact.

Other approaches he may mention:

  • Excavate and remove ("dig and haul") — excavate the contaminated soil and truck it to a licensed disposal facility. Fast, certain, and expensive.
  • Soil vapor extraction — pull volatile contaminants out of the soil as vapor through wells.
  • Capping / containment — leave the contamination in place and seal it under an impermeable barrier so it can't migrate or be contacted.
  • Soil washing / chemical treatment / thermal treatment — on-site processing of the soil.

Easy matching pair: bio = bugs, phyto = plants.

Clearing and grubbing

Two different things, and a classic matching pair:

  • Clearing = removing what's above ground — trees, brush, vegetation.
  • Grubbing = removing what's below ground — roots, stumps, buried organic material. Organics must come out because they decompose and cause settlement.
Compaction — which roller for which soil? (near-certain exam question)
Memorize this pairing

Granular (cohesionless) soil → VIBRATORY roller. It compacts by dead weight plus vibration.
Cohesive soil → SHEEPSFOOT / PAD roller. It compacts by dead weight plus a kneading (manipulating) action.

Why it works that way: sand and gravel are loose individual grains with no bond between them. Vibration shakes them so they rearrange and nest tightly together — vibration is what densifies granular soil. Clay is a sticky, plastic mass; vibration just passes through it. What clay needs is to be physically kneaded and remolded, so the protruding "feet" of a sheepsfoot roller punch into the layer and knead it from within, working out air voids.

EquipmentBest forAction
Vibratory (smooth drum) rollerGranular — sand, gravel, crushed stoneDead weight + vibration
Sheepsfoot / pad rollerCohesive — clay, silty clayDead weight + kneading
Static smooth drum rollerFinishing surfaces, asphaltDead weight only
Plate compactor / jumping jack (rammer)Tight spots — trenches, around foundations, behind wallsImpact + vibration

Lift

A lift is one layer of fill placed and compacted at a time — typically around 6 to 12 inches. Fill is placed in lifts because a compactor can only densify soil to a limited depth; dumping three feet of fill and rolling the top leaves the bottom loose, which settles later.

What is a water wagon used for?

A water wagon (water truck) spreads water over the fill to bring the soil to its optimum moisture content — the water content at which that soil compacts to its maximum density for a given compactive effort. Too dry and the particles won't slide into place; too wet and the water occupies space the soil should. It's also used for dust control on haul roads.

A sieve is the screen with a standard mesh used to separate a soil sample by particle size; the number is roughly the openings per inch, so a higher number is a finer screen. The No. 200 sieve is the dividing line between coarse-grained and fine-grained soil.

Which predominates in a granular soil? Gravel and sand.
Which predominates in a cohesive soil? Clay — clay is what makes a soil cohesive.

Loam is a natural mixture of sand, silt and clay (usually with organic matter) — good topsoil for growing things, but not a structural material; it is stripped off before construction.

What is cohesion? What is plasticity? Why does only clay have them?

Cohesion

The attraction between soil particles that makes them stick together, giving the soil strength independent of any confining pressure. It's why a lump of clay holds together in your hand and why a clay bank can stand in a near-vertical face — while dry sand immediately slumps to its angle of repose.

Plasticity

The ability of a soil to be molded or deformed without cracking or crumbling, and to retain that new shape after the deforming force is removed. Rolling a moist clay into a thread and having it hold together is plasticity.

Why clay possesses them and the other particles do not

This is a "why" question in the study guide, so be ready to explain it, not just name it:

  • Clay particles are extremely small and flat/plate-like, rather than bulky grains. That shape gives them an enormous surface area relative to their volume.
  • Those surfaces carry an electrical charge. Water is a polar molecule, so it is attracted to and held as a thin film on and between the clay plates.
  • That adsorbed water film, together with the electrochemical attraction between the plates, bonds the particles to one another. The plates can also slide over one another and stay in the new position — which is exactly what plasticity is.
  • Sand, gravel, cobbles and even silt are comparatively huge, bulky, rounded or angular grains that are chemically inert. There is no meaningful surface charge and no attraction between them — they behave as loose individual grains. Their strength comes only from friction and interlock under confinement, not cohesion.
Careful

Silt is fine-grained but it is NOT cohesive. It passes the No. 200 sieve like clay does, and it drains as badly as clay, but it has no real cohesion or plasticity — silt particles are just very small bulky grains, not charged plates. Damp silt can seem to hold together from surface tension alone ("apparent cohesion"), which disappears as it dries or saturates. Telling silt from clay is exactly what the lab tests below are for.

The three lab tests — dilatancy, thread, and dry strength

These are the visual/manual tests from Lab 2 for deciding whether the fines in a sample are silt or clay. In every one of them, silt reacts, clay doesn't — except for dry strength, where clay is the strong one.

A way to remember it

Clay is the tough one. It holds a thread, it's hard when dry, and it doesn't react to shaking. Silt is the weak, reactive one. It crumbles, powders, and goes shiny when you shake it.

What effect does the presence of fines have on a soil's drainage?

Fines drastically reduce permeability, so drainage becomes poor. Water moves through soil in the voids between particles; in a clean sand or gravel the voids are large and interconnected and water flows freely. Silt and clay particles are so small that they fill and choke those void passages, and clay additionally holds water on its surfaces. The result is water that cannot get through.

Consequences on a construction site

  • Surface water ponds instead of soaking in. This is exactly why the dewatering slide says surface water from rainfall is especially a problem "if soil at the site doesn't drain well (silty and clayey soils)."
  • Muddy, unworkable site conditions — trafficking becomes difficult.
  • Frost heave — silty soils in particular draw water up by capillary action and form ice lenses that lift whatever is above them.
  • Volume change — clay shrinks when it dries and swells when it wets, which moves foundations and slabs.
  • Loss of bearing capacity when saturated, and long-term settlement (consolidation), because water is squeezed out of clay very slowly under load.
  • Harder to compact — moisture content becomes critical and a wet clay may be impossible to compact at all without drying or treating it.

Nice symmetry worth knowing for the dewatering questions: silty and clayey soils cause a surface water problem (it can't soak in). Clean granular soils — sands, sand and gravel mixtures — cause a groundwater problem (it flows in freely when you dig below the water table).

7 · Stabilizing the sides of an excavation

Two fundamental choices. If you have room, lay the sides back. If you don't, hold them up with sheeting.

Unsupported excavation — sloping, benching, and the angle of repose
  • Sloping — cut the sides back at a safe angle so the soil stands on its own. Simple and cheap, but it requires a lot of room, and the deeper the hole the more room it eats.
  • Benching — cut the side into a series of horizontal steps or levels rather than one continuous slope. Same principle as sloping; used in cohesive soils.

Angle of repose

The steepest angle, measured from the horizontal, at which a soil will stand on its own without sliding or sloughing off. It varies with soil type and moisture content — granular soils generally need a flatter slope; cohesive soils can stand steeper because cohesion holds them together. Saturating a soil lowers its angle of repose, which is why a slope that stood fine all summer can fail after heavy rain.

OSHA classifies soils as Type A (most stable, cohesive), Type B, and Type C (least stable — granular, submerged) and specifies a maximum allowable slope for each, with Type C requiring the flattest cut.

When sloping isn't an option

On a tight urban site — adjacent buildings, streets and sidewalks right at the property line — there is simply no room to lay the sides back, and no right to undermine the neighbor's property. That's when you must use sheeting.

The sheeting systems

Sheeting is the vertical material that physically retains the soil at the face of the excavation.

SystemHow it worksNotes
Soldier piles (soldier beams) & lagging H-piles are driven, or set in drilled holes, at intervals around the perimeter before excavation. Can be incorporated as the permanent basement wall of the finished building. The go-to for deep excavations on tight urban sites next to existing buildings. Most expensive.

An H-pile is simply a structural steel section shaped like an H (a wide flange), well suited to being driven; it's what soldier piles are usually made of.

The steps to build a slurry wall — know these in order

The study guide says: "Know the steps usually required to build a slurry wall." This is one of the most likely places for a sequencing or fill-in question.

  1. Build guide walls — two shallow parallel concrete walls at the surface that define the line and width of the trench and keep its top edges from collapsing.
  2. Excavate a narrow trench in panels (sections), using a clamshell bucket hung from a crane (or a hydromill), rather than digging the whole wall at once.
  3. Keep the trench filled with bentonite slurry as excavation proceeds. Bentonite is a clay that forms a dense, viscous fluid; its hydrostatic pressure pushes outward on the trench walls and holds them open, preventing caving. It also forms a filter cake on the soil face. The slurry is the whole trick of the method.
  4. Lower a prefabricated reinforcing steel cage into the slurry-filled trench with a crane.
  5. Place concrete through a tremie pipe, from the bottom up. The concrete is heavier than the slurry, so it fills from the bottom and displaces the slurry upward and out of the trench.
  6. Recover, clean and reuse the displaced slurry in the next panel.
  7. Repeat panel by panel (typically alternating panels, then filling in between) until the wall is continuous around the site.
  8. Excavate inside the completed wall, installing bracing as you go down (Topic 8).

The tremie pipe — why it's used

A tremie is a pipe with a funnel at the top used to place concrete underwater, below ground level, or through slurry. It's lowered to the bottom and the discharge end is kept embedded in the fresh concrete so the concrete flows out at the bottom and rises, never falling through the water or slurry. Advantages from the slide:

  • Prevents segregation — placing bottom-up stops the aggregate separating from the cement paste, giving a uniform mix
  • Controls flow — steady, controlled placement
  • Reduces air pockets — slow continuous placement minimizes entrained air, which would weaken the concrete
  • Avoids cement washout — the cement isn't washed out of the mix by the surrounding water or slurry

8 · Shoring (bracing) the sheeting

Why bracing is needed at all: once the sheeting or slurry wall is in place, excavation begins for the building's substructure and foundation. Removing the soil from one side of the wall causes an imbalance of lateral soil pressure — there's still earth pushing on the back and now nothing pushing back on the front. As the excavation goes deeper the imbalance grows, so bracing (also called shoring) must be installed to support the wall.

The three types of shoring
TypeHow it worksWhen you'd use it / trade-off
Crosslot bracing Horizontal struts span all the way across the excavation from one wall to the opposite wall, bearing against wales on each face. The two walls literally prop each other apart. Works when the excavation is narrow enough to span. Simple and reliable, but the struts fill the hole and get in the way of excavating and building.
Rakers & heel blocks Inclined struts (rakers) run diagonally from the wall down to the excavation floor. The bottom of each raker bears on a concrete heel block (kicker block) cast on the excavation floor to take the thrust. For excavations too wide to span crosslot. Requires excavating the middle first and leaving soil berms at the perimeter, then working around. Still obstructs the perimeter.
Tiebacks Tensioned steel anchors drilled at a downward angle through the wall into the soil behind it, and anchored there with grout. Leaves the excavation completely clear — the big advantage, since nothing obstructs excavation or construction. Needs the right to install anchors under adjacent property, and suitable soil to anchor into.

The two components to know

  • Wale (whaler) — a horizontal beam running along the face of the sheeting that collects the soil pressure from the whole wall and distributes it into the struts or tiebacks.
  • Strut — a crosslot brace; a compression member spanning the excavation.

Excavation sequence with crosslot bracing

You never excavate to full depth and then brace — the wall would fail first. It's alternating:

  1. Walls (sheeting or slurry wall) installed first, from the surface.
  2. Excavate down a limited depth.
  3. Install the first (top) level of crosslot braces.
  4. Excavate deeper to the next level.
  5. Install the next level of braces. Repeat to the bottom.

The same top-down, brace-as-you-go logic applies to rakers and tiebacks.

How is a tieback installed? (three steps — very likely a question)
  1. A hole is drilled at a downward angle through the sheeting or slurry wall and into the soil behind it.
  2. The tieback is inserted and grout is injected under pressure into the soil to form an anchor. The pressurized grout forms a bulb that grips the soil, out beyond the zone of soil that would slide if the wall failed.
  3. After the grout hardens, the tendons are stressed using a hydraulic jack and locked off against a bearing plate at the wall face. Pre-stressing them means the wall is actively held back rather than waiting to move.

The tendons are high-strength steel strands or bars. Tiebacks can be installed at multiple levels down the face of a deep excavation, and are used with both soldier piles & lagging and slurry walls. They're sometimes combined with rakers on the same job.

Rock excavation
  • If the rock is soft, it can be ripped — torn up with a ripper shank pulled behind a large dozer.
  • If it is hard, it must be blasted out using dynamite (which requires a blasting permit — see Topic 2).
  • Sheeting is generally not required in rock — the rock stands on its own.
  • If rock along the vertical sides is fractured, it can be stabilized by grout injection or rock anchors (similar to tiebacks).
Dewatering — sources of water, the two systems, and what to consider

Dewatering is the process of removing water from an excavation to provide a dry working platform.

Two sources of water

  • Surface water from rainfall — especially a problem if the soil at the site doesn't drain well (silty and clayey soils).
  • Groundwater that seeps into the excavation when digging below the water table — a problem in cohesionless soils: sands, sand & gravel mixtures, because water flows through them freely.

Two types of dewatering system

More on well points: they can be placed inside or outside the actual excavation, and their size and spacing depend on the volume of water. Besides keeping the hole dry, they stabilize the soil by eliminating the pressure of seeping water.

Considerations when dewatering

  • Water discharge — what do you do with all that water? (Permits, treatment, where it goes.)
  • Surrounding water table drawdown — how is the pumping affecting the groundwater level outside the site? Do recharge wells need to be installed to keep the water level outside the excavation at normal levels? (Lowering it under a neighbor can cause their building to settle.)
  • Soil contamination migration & treatment — is the groundwater carrying contamination with it? Does the water need to be treated?
  • Reliability of the pumping system — is there a warning system or backup should the pumps stop working? (The slide showing a flooded excavation with a worker waist-deep makes the point.)

9 · Site exploration

Why we do it at all: before a building foundation can be designed, the subsurface conditions at the site must be known. The foundation type depends on the magnitude of the structural loads to be supported and the strength and settlement characteristics of the underlying soil. If loads are high and/or the soil is weak, the foundation cannot rest on the soil and must be taken down to bedrock — in which case the condition and strength of the bedrock must be determined too.

Test pits and borings let you determine: the kinds of soil present, the condition of each layer, and the location of the groundwater table. How many you take and how deep depends on the soil encountered and the magnitude of the loads.

Test pits — equipment, depth, and what gets recorded
  • Equipment: a backhoe.
  • Depth is limited by the size of the backhoe, so this method is used when strong soil is anticipated at a fairly shallow depth below the surface.
  • The sides of a pit may require sheeting and shoring to comply with OSHA standards for safety.
  • The pit may need to be dewatered to allow dry working conditions while the soil is examined and samples retrieved.
  • A log is kept showing the depth at which different soils are encountered and the location of the groundwater table, if found. Each different soil layer is described in detail after thorough visual examination.
  • Hand-held devices can be poked into the soil to estimate its strength. Any large obstructions encountered are noted, as well as the relative difficulty of excavating the hole.
  • Samples are cut out by hand, carefully packed and shipped to the laboratory for testing.
  • The pit should be dug to a considerable depth below the bearing layer to determine if weaker layers are present below it. If a suitable soil is found at shallow depth allowing spread footings, pits are carried to at least twice that depth (or more). A weak underlying layer can cause considerable long-term settlement.
Borings — the drilling process

For deep applications, borings are generally taken.

  • A hole is drilled using a drill rod and drill bit. To advance the drill rod, a rotary and chopping action is used (rotating plus up-and-down motion of the bit).
  • Jetting — water is forced out of the end of the drill bit into the soil to help part the soil and advance the drill rod.
  • Casing — an outer pipe may be placed in the hole to keep it open while further drilling is done, preventing caving (especially in granular soils). The casing is large enough for the drill rod to fit inside it, and can be driven into the pre-drilled hole using a drop weight.
  • Soil types and layer changes are identified by examining the soil cuttings that rise to the surface in the wash water.
  • Sampling is done when a layer change is surmised from a change in driving resistance and in the color of the wash water. The bit at the end is removed and a sampling spoon is attached.
  • Samples can be taken at any depth by removing the drill bit and replacing it with a sampling device.

The Standard Penetration Test and the Standard Penetration Number
The highest-probability fill-in on the exam

The split spoon is driven by a 140-pound drop weight free falling 30 inches onto the drill rod. The Standard Penetration Number (N) is the total number of blows for the LAST 12 INCHES of driving.

The procedure

  1. The split spoon is attached to the drill rod at the sampling depth.
  2. It is driven into the soil by the 140 lb weight free-falling 30 inches.
  3. A driving record — the boring log — is kept showing the number of blows for each six inches of driving. This is the blow count.
  4. The spoon is usually driven a total of 18 inches (sometimes 24). The first 6 inches is disturbed material and is discarded from the count.
  5. The Standard Penetration Number is determined by totaling the number of blows for the last 12 inches of driving. Sometimes the log will only show those blows.
  6. The whole process of using the drop weight and reporting the standard penetration number is referred to as the Standard Penetration Test (SPT).

What does the number tell us about the soil?

N is used to determine the consistency (or relative density) of the soil and to estimate its bearing capacity — how much load it can safely support. This is an initial step in the design of the foundation system. A high N means dense/stiff, strong soil; a low N means loose/soft, weak soil.

Worked example: blows of 4, 7 and 9 for the three 6-inch increments. Discard the first. N = 7 + 9 = 16. If it's a cohesionless soil, that's medium relative density; if cohesive, very stiff.

What information is recorded on a boring log?

A boring log is the record of one hole. It shows:

  • The boring number and its location on the site, plus the date and the ground surface elevation
  • The depth at which each different soil layer is encountered, and the thickness of each
  • A description / classification of each soil layer (e.g. "brown medium to fine SAND, trace silt")
  • The blow counts for each 6-inch increment and the resulting N value at each sample depth
  • The sample number and type (split spoon or tube) and the depth it came from
  • The depth to the groundwater table, if encountered
  • The depth to bedrock / refusal, and rock coring information if rock was cored
  • Drilling details — casing used, drilling method, the rig and the driller
What is a soil profile, and who uses this information?

Soil profile

A vertical cross-section through the site, drawn by plotting several boring logs side by side at their correct locations and horizontal spacing, then connecting and interpolating the matching layers between them. Where a boring log tells you about one point, the profile shows you how the strata run across the site — whether a layer thickens, thins, slopes or disappears.

What's displayed on it: each soil stratum and its description, the elevation and thickness of each, the groundwater table, the top of bedrock surface, and the boring locations with their N values marked at the sampled depths.

Boring log = one hole, in detail. Soil profile = the whole site, in section. That distinction is exactly the kind of thing a matching question is made of.

Who uses the boring logs and soil profile?

  • The geotechnical engineer (soils/foundation engineer) above all — they interpret the data and recommend the foundation type and allowable bearing pressure. The study guide names this one directly.
  • The structural engineer — designs the actual foundation from that recommendation.
  • The architect — for basement depth and how the building sits on the site.
  • The civil engineer — site drainage and retaining walls.
  • The contractor / estimator — to price excavation, dewatering, shoring and rock removal, and to plan means and methods. The logs tell them whether they'll hit rock or water.
  • The owner — because foundation conditions can drive major cost decisions.
One more detail from the handout

Subsurface investigation for residential buildings is usually very minimal because of the relatively light loads imposed; samples are rarely taken for testing on those projects. A more extensive investigation is only warranted if the site is found to be underlain by a very problematic soil like peat.
