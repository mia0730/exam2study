1.1 · What a foundation does

The definition, in his words

Purpose of a foundation: provide adequate support for the building by "safely" transferring building loads to the underlying soil or bedrock.

The three major building parts

Top to bottom — and notice this ties straight back to Exam 1 vocabulary:

SuperstructureEverything above ground
SubstructureThe below-ground portion of the building
FoundationThe lowest part — what actually delivers the load into the soil or rock

The two basic types

Shallow foundation

Used when adequate bearing is directly below the substructure. The good soil is already there — just spread the load onto it.

Deep foundation

Used when unsuitable soil beneath the substructure must be bypassed to reach competent soil or bedrock. You have to reach down past the bad stuff.

The three foundation requirements

Safeguard against structural failure

The foundation must safely support anticipated loads. The soil or rock supporting the foundation cannot be overloaded.

Tolerable amount of settlement

Post-construction settlement of foundations supported by soil can be significant and occurs over time. The total amount must be within acceptable limits.

Technically & economically feasible

Geotechnical engineers must find the most cost-effective solution for the soil conditions at the site and the anticipated building loads.

1.2 · Types of settlement

TypeWhat happensHow bad?
No settlementThe building stays putIdeal, but rarely real
Uniform settlementThe whole building sinks the same amount, evenlyTolerable — nothing is being racked out of square
Differential settlementParts of the building settle different amounts, so the frame distortsThe most troublesome. This is what cracks walls and jams doors

The slide's example: the Leaning Tower of Pisa — one of the most famous examples of differential settlement. If it had settled uniformly it would just be a slightly shorter tower.

1.3 · Shallow foundations

How they work: by spreading the building loads over a large enough area so as not to exceed the soil's bearing capacity.

Bearing capacity = the maximum allowable pressure that the soil can safely support.

There also cannot be soft, compressible layers beneath the bearing layer, or you get settlement problems after construction. (Exactly why Exam 1 said to dig test pits to at least twice the footing depth.)

Presumptive bearing capacities

Building codes give presumptive (assumed) bearing capacities based on the type of soil or rock and its condition. These can be used for preliminary design. From the Massachusetts State Building Code:

MaterialCapacity
Crystalline bedrock (e.g. sound granite)100 tsf
Sedimentary rock (e.g. hard shale)20 tsf
Dense sand & gravel6 tsf
Hard clayey silt or clay4 tsf
Loose sand & gravel2 tsf
Medium stiff clayey silt or clay1 tsf

1 tsf = 1 ton per square foot = 2,000 psf. Know this conversion — a question could give you tsf and expect psf.

Three other requirements

  • Should be constructed on undisturbed soil or engineered (compacted) fill
  • The base of the foundation must be below the frost line in cold regions
  • Must not be constructed on frozen soil

Shallow foundation types

Two families: footings and slabs on grade (SOG).

Footing typeWhat it is
Column footing
also called an isolated column footing or a spread footing		Each column is supported by its own concrete pad
Wall footing
also called a strip footing
or continuous wall footing		A long continuous pad running under a wall
Combined footingOne pad supporting two or more columns
Stepped footingUsed where the supporting soil slopes — the footing steps down in stages to stay at a consistent depth
The sentence that explains all of it

"A footing is a pad that safely distributes the building weight to the supporting soil. The weaker the soil, the larger the pad." That second sentence is the whole calculation in words.

Slab on grade with thickened edges

  • A single layer of concrete, several inches thick, supported by the ground
  • Poured thicker at the edges to form an integral footing to support perimeter walls
  • Uses: residential & light commercial buildings in areas with little to no frost

One construction detail worth knowing

On a continuous wall footing, the concrete is often cast directly against the soil with no forms. The bottom rebar is held up off the soil with concrete brick. Rebar bent into an L shape is attached to the bottom bars and extends upward — these are dowels, and they tie the wall reinforcing to the footing. Dowels extend up out of finished footings as the means of attaching walls to be built later.

1.4 · Sizing a footing Calculation

The formula
Footing Area = Load on Footing ÷ Soil Bearing Capacity
For a square footing: Side = √Area

Two steps the slides spell out before you can use it:

  • Determine the load on the footing — the total weight it will support, including dead load, live load and any other applicable loads. (For a deck: the weight of the deck structure plus expected loads like people or snow.)
  • Determine the soil bearing capacity — the maximum pressure the soil can withstand without failing. Usually obtained from soil tests and geotechnical reports.

Then choose a shape from the area: square (take the square root of the area to get each side), rectangular, or circular (use the area to calculate the diameter).

His two worked examples

Example A — the deck post

A deck post carries 6,000 lbs. The soil has a bearing capacity of 2,500 psf.

Footing Area = 6,000 lbs ÷ 2,500 psf = 2.4 sf
Square footing side = √2.4 = 1.55 ft per side

Example B — the class example

Column load = 60,000 lbs. Soil material = Soft Rock. What size square footing is required?

Look up Soft Rock → 440 psf
Area = 60,000 lbs ÷ 440 psf = 136.36 sf
Side = √136.36 = 11.68 ft × 11.68 ft
Careful — there's a typo on his answer slide

His slide writes the area as 136.27 and then takes the root of 136.37. Neither is quite right: 60,000 ÷ 440 = 136.36. But his final answer, 11.68 ft, is correct — √136.36 = 11.677.

So: do the arithmetic properly and you'll match his final answer anyway. If his answer key shows 136.27 as an intermediate step, don't second-guess yourself — you're not wrong, and the side dimension lands in the same place either way.

Try it

Enter any load and capacity — it works out the area and the square side, showing the arithmetic. Start by re-entering 60,000 and 440 to confirm you get 136.27 and 11.68.

Two traps

Units. If the table gives tsf and the load is in pounds, convert first — 1 tsf = 2,000 psf. Square root. The formula gives you an area; the question usually wants the side dimension, so don't stop at the division.

1.5 · Deep foundations

Used when the unsuitable soil beneath the substructure must be bypassed to reach competent soil or bedrock.

The five types he lists

PilesDriven  ·  Cast-in-place (augered)
CaissonsAlso called drilled piers. The belled caisson flares out at the bottom
Pressure Injected Footings (PIFs)Also called a bulb pile or a Franki pile. PIFs were used for WIT's own student apartment building at 525 Huntington Avenue
Load Bearing Elements (LBEs)Also called barrettes

How piles carry building loads

Gravity loads on a building — dead, live & snow — act in a downward direction imposing compressive forces on the foundation. Compression piles transmit these forces to the supporting layers, in one of three ways:

End bearing

Resistance at the pile tip (end) from dense/stiff soil or firm bedrock. The pile works like a column standing on rock.

Friction

Resistance between the sides of the pile and the soil — skin friction. The pile grips the soil along its whole length.

…or a combination of both.

Compression piles vs. uplift piles

  • Occasionally piles must be designed to resist uplift — forces that tend to pull the piles out of the ground. These piles are in tension.
  • Piles designed to resist uplift are called uplift piles, tension piles or anchor piles.
  • Wind can cause overturning of a structure — on a transmission tower, the piles on one side go into compression while those on the other side go into tension.
  • Hydrostatic forces caused by groundwater can cause uplift on a substructure, requiring uplift piles to hold it down.
  • A pile with a straight shaft can only resist uplift through skin friction. A bulb pile has the added advantage of the bulb at the bottom, which acts like an anchor to resist uplift.

Materials for driven piles

  • Steel — H-piles or steel pipe
  • Precast concrete
  • Wood / timber

Site cast augered piles

The cast-in-place alternative to driving. Note how closely this mirrors the slurry wall from Exam 1 — bottom-up placement again.

Auger the hole

The hole is made using an auger.

Fill with concrete or grout

Concrete is a mixture of sand, gravel, cement & water. Grout is similar but with no gravel. It is forced through a pipe in the stem of the auger.

Fill from the bottom up

The hole fills from the bottom up as the auger is slowly lifted out — which is what keeps the hole from collapsing.

Drop in the cage

A pre-fabricated rebar cage is placed through the wet concrete or grout to strengthen the pile.

Load Bearing Elements (barrettes)

  • Constructed of cast-in-place concrete reinforced with rebar
  • Excavation & construction of each LBE is done using modified clamshell & slurry wall techniques — the same method as the Exam 1 slurry wall
  • Constructed in different shapes: rectangular, L, X or H
  • Each barrette is usually designed to support one column

1.6 · Pile drivers

Pile drivers are of 2 types: Impact or Vibratory.

Impact hammers

  • Use the force of a falling ram to drive piles
  • Diesel operated hammers are most common
  • The ram strikes a steel helmet placed on top of the pile
  • Wooden cushions inside the helmet soften the blow so the pile top (the butt) is not damaged

Vibratory hammers

  • Best suited to granular soils, where they are very efficient (fast)
  • Vibration causes the soil around the pile to liquefy; the pile sinks
  • Rotating eccentric weights inside the driver body cause the vibrations
  • Can be hung from a crane or attached to an excavator boom
  • Can also be used to extract old piles
Carry-over from Exam 1

Vibratory equipment works in granular soil. Same as the vibratory roller for compacting sand and gravel on Exam 1 — vibration rearranges loose grains, and does nothing useful in clay. One idea, two exams.

The Nashville Coliseum case study

A 67,000-seat sports coliseum in Nashville, TN (now Nissan Stadium), with deep foundations:

  • 3,500 driven piles; 12x53 H piles with end bearing
  • Pile length varied from 25' to 75'
  • 3 pile drivers with diesel powered hammers
  • Driving rate: 20–25 piles per day per rig
  • Driving tolerance: 6 inches maximum

1.7 · Pile caps & grade beams

Pile cap

  • Ties piles in a cluster together
  • Distributes the column load to the cluster
  • Used under a column, or under a wall
  • Clusters come in different arrangements — 2, 3 (triangular), 4, 9 piles, etc.

Grade beam

  • Spans between pile caps
  • Another way to support basement walls
  • Cast against the ground, but carries wall loads back to the pile clusters — it doesn't rely on the soil under it

1.8 · Pile load testing

Purpose of a pile load test: to determine or confirm the installation criteria and the design capacity of piles.

  • The design strength of a pile is calculated by the geotechnical engineer from data like blow counts on boring logs — the Exam 1 SPT data feeding straight into Exam 2.
  • Testing is needed to confirm that a pile can actually achieve the predicted capacity when driven according to the specs the engineer has written.

The Static Load Test — the most commonly used test

Install the test pile

Apply load in increments

Load is applied to the pile in increments, usually up to twice the design capacity.

Remove load in increments

Measure settlement at every step

Pile settlement is measured and recorded for each increment using dial gauges mounted on reference beams.

Two ways to generate the load:

  • Load platform — a platform of weights on cross beams bearing on the test pile through cribbing
  • Anchor piles — a hydraulic jack pushes down on the test pile against test beams held down by anchor piles on either side

1.9 · Reading a pile driving log

The sample is an H-Pile Driving Log from Haley & Aldrich for the Massachusetts College of Art New Residence Hall in Boston (GC: Suffolk Construction; pile contractor: H&L Pile). It's the pile-driving equivalent of Exam 1's boring log.

What's recorded on it

SectionWhat it captures
HeaderPile number and its column location on the grid (e.g. D-1), project, client, GC, pile contractor, field rep
Sections & lengthsEach section of pile, the date driven, and its measured length — this pile was two 75 ft sections spliced, total length of pile 151 ft
ElevationsApprox. ground surface el., initial top el., tip elevation, cut-off elevation — how deep the pile ended up and where it gets cut
Heave measurementWhether driving nearby piles pushed this one back up. Movement of 0.00 means no heave. Heave means it must be redriven
The main tableDepth (ft) vs. blows per foot, all the way down. Watch the numbers climb: ~2 blows/ft near the surface, 6, then 10, 12, 24, 36 as the pile reaches denser material
Remarks checkboxesRejected · added due to mislocated pile · added due to broken pile · added/deleted due to design change · possibly broken · stopped driving to splice · stopped driving at ___ ft
Pile dataType — End Bearing or Friction — size, and design capacity in tons
Hammer dataType, rated energy and measured energy in ft-lbs
Final acceptancePile Accepted or Pile Rejected
The single most important line on the log

At the bottom: "Blows for final 6 in.: 8, 9, 10, 11, 11, 15 — Blows/in. Avg.: 10.6 · Req. Avg.: 8.0"

This is the driving resistance, or set. The spec says the pile must take at least 8 blows per inch over the final inches before it counts as having reached capacity. This pile averaged 10.6, which exceeds 8.0 — so it's accepted. Rising blow counts mean rising resistance, which means the pile has found competent bearing.

The parallel to learn: on a boring log, blow count (N) tells you how strong the soil is. On a pile driving log, blows per inch tells you whether the pile has reached soil strong enough to carry it. Both are "how hard was it to push steel into the ground."
