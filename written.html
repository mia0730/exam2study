1 · Differences between shallow and deep foundations

Model answer

The purpose of any foundation is to provide adequate support for a building by safely transferring the building's loads to the underlying soil or bedrock. Whether that is done with a shallow or a deep foundation depends entirely on where competent bearing material is located.

A shallow foundation is used when adequate bearing is found directly below the substructure. It works by spreading the building loads over a large enough area that the pressure does not exceed the soil's bearing capacity, which is the maximum allowable pressure the soil can safely support. Because the load is simply spread onto the soil immediately beneath the building, the required area is calculated by dividing the load on the footing by the soil's bearing capacity — meaning the weaker the soil, the larger the pad must be. Shallow foundations include footings — column or spread footings, wall or strip footings, combined footings, and stepped footings on sloping ground — as well as slabs on grade. They must be built on undisturbed soil or engineered compacted fill, must bear below the frost line in cold regions, and must never be placed on frozen soil. There also cannot be soft, compressible layers beneath the bearing layer, because those would cause settlement after construction.

A deep foundation is used when unsuitable soil beneath the substructure must be bypassed in order to reach competent soil or bedrock. Rather than spreading the load near the surface, a deep foundation carries it down through the poor material to a stratum that can support it. Deep foundations include piles — either driven or cast in place by augering — caissons or drilled piers, pressure injected footings (also called bulb piles or Franki piles), and load bearing elements known as barrettes. Piles transfer their loads in one of three ways: by end bearing, which is resistance at the pile tip from dense or stiff soil or firm bedrock; by friction, which is skin friction between the sides of the pile and the surrounding soil; or by a combination of both. Because individual piles are slender, they are usually grouped in clusters and tied together by a pile cap, which distributes the column load to the cluster, and grade beams may span between pile caps to support basement walls.

In short, the difference is one of depth and mechanism. A shallow foundation spreads the load out onto good soil that is already there; a deep foundation reaches down past bad soil to find good material further below. Shallow foundations are simpler and far less expensive, so they are used wherever the soil conditions permit, and deep foundations are used when those conditions do not.

2 · The concrete hydration process and its importance

Model answer

Hydration is the chemical reaction between Portland cement and water that causes concrete to harden. When water is added to the mix, the cement reacts with it to form a gel that hardens and binds the other ingredients — the sand, gravel and crushed stone — together into a single rocklike material. Portland cement is therefore properly thought of as the glue in concrete, and hydration is the reaction that activates it. The reaction is exothermic, meaning it gives off heat as it proceeds.

The single most important thing to understand about hydration is that concrete hardens by curing, not by drying out. The water in the mix is not simply evaporating away; it is being chemically consumed. If moisture is lost prematurely, hydration is incomplete, because the chemical reaction that causes the concrete to harden stops when there is no moisture left to react with the cement. Critically, concrete cannot recover from premature drying out — once the reaction has stopped, re-wetting the concrete will not restore the strength that was lost, and the ultimate strength is permanently reduced.

This is why curing is treated as a real construction operation rather than an afterthought. The problem is most severe in flatwork such as slabs, because the large surface area exposed to the air is prone to rapid drying, which causes excessive shrinkage and cracking at the surface. Moisture is retained either by covering the concrete — with polyethylene sheeting, saturated burlap, canvas, moist straw, roofing paper, moist sand, or a chemical membrane known as a curing compound — or by actively providing moisture through misting, fogging, or ponding the slab surface.

Temperature matters as much as moisture, because it controls the rate of the reaction. The ideal curing temperature for concrete is between 50 and 85 degrees Fahrenheit. Below 50 degrees the curing process slows and the concrete takes longer to gain its ultimate strength. Temperatures slightly below freezing can be tolerated if the concrete is covered with curing blankets to trap the heat of hydration, but temperatures well below freezing are dangerous: water that has not yet reacted with the cement will freeze and become unavailable for hydration, and because water expands when it freezes it creates internal pressures within the concrete, greatly reducing the ultimate strength. Above 85 degrees, water is lost rapidly to evaporation, hydration is again incomplete, and heat build-up within the concrete leads to additional shrinkage cracking.

In summary, hydration is what gives concrete its strength, and it will only proceed as long as adequate moisture and a reasonable temperature are maintained. Everything the contractor does during curing — covering, misting, blanketing, scheduling pours for cooler parts of the day, or using a retarder in hot weather and an accelerator or Type III cement in cold weather — is done to protect that reaction.

3 · General steps in constructing and finishing a concrete slab

Model answer

Constructing a concrete slab on grade divides into two phases: the preparation that happens before any concrete arrives, and the placing and finishing operations that follow.

Preparation. The work begins by stripping the topsoil and loam from the area, since organic material decomposes over time and would cause the slab to settle. The subgrade is then prepared, which may require additional excavation or the placement of compacted fill to bring the ground up to the required grade. Any underground utilities are installed at this point, while there is still open access. Crushed stone or another permeable fill is then placed, serving two purposes: it promotes good drainage beneath the slab, and it acts as structural fill to help support the loads on the slab. Edge formwork is installed next, with the top of the forms set to the proper elevation and with adequate bracing. Material for the joints is installed, followed by a moisture barrier of plastic film to prevent ground moisture from wicking up through the slab. Finally the reinforcing is installed — light gauge welded wire fabric for a lightly loaded slab, or heavy gauge welded wire fabric or reinforcing bars for a heavily loaded one — supported on chairs and bolsters so that it ends up with proper concrete cover.

Placing and finishing. With the preparation complete, there are five steps, and the same five are followed for slabs above grade.

  1. Place and spread the concrete. It is delivered by chute, wheelbarrow, buggy, bucket and crane, conveyor belt or pump, and spread with concrete rakes, also called mud rakes or come-a-longs. Concrete must not be allowed to free fall more than four feet, or it will segregate.
  2. Consolidate. This is done to ensure the concrete completely fills the forms and that entrapped air is removed, using hand-held vibrators or vibratory screeds. Too little consolidation produces honeycomb; excessive vibration causes segregation.
  3. Screed, also called strike-off. The surface is levelled and brought even with the top of the forms using a straight edge, a vibratory screed or a roller screed. The coarse aggregate must be worked down before the concrete begins to harden. Screeding leaves ridges and large voids behind.
  4. Bull float. Done immediately after screeding with a bull float and magnesium hand floats, this brings up the paste, fills the large surface voids and begins to create a smooth surface.
  5. Final finish. This cannot begin until most of the bleed water has evaporated from the surface and the concrete can support the weight of a worker with only a slight indentation of about one eighth of an inch, which may be several hours after bull floating. Floating is done first, with a power trowel fitted with float blades and with hand floats along the edges; this drives the small aggregate into the slab, brings more water to the surface, removes small waves and fills small holes. Troweling follows, using steel power trowels with finishing blades or hand trowels, to produce a smooth, dense surface — or, where a skid-resistant surface is wanted, a steel or fiber broom is dragged across it instead to produce a broom finish. Edges are rounded with an edging tool, and control joints are tooled in at this time, or saw cut a day or two later while the concrete is still green.

Finally, the slab must be cured, by covering it or by providing moisture, so that hydration can continue and the concrete reaches its design strength.

4 · Causes, effects and prevention of differential settlement

Model answer

Settlement is the downward movement of a building as the soil beneath it compresses under load, and some settlement is normal and expected. Post-construction settlement of foundations supported by soil can be significant and occurs over time, which is why one of the three requirements of any foundation is that the total settlement remains within tolerable limits. Settlement comes in three forms: no settlement, uniform settlement in which the entire building moves down by the same amount, and differential settlement, in which different parts of the building settle by different amounts. Differential settlement is by far the most troublesome of the three, because uniform settlement simply lowers the building while differential settlement distorts and racks its frame.

Causes. Differential settlement occurs whenever the support conditions are not the same under every part of the building. Common causes include variation in the soil across the site, such as one portion of the footprint bearing on dense sand and another on soft clay; soft, compressible layers located beneath the bearing layer at some locations but not others, which is why test pits and borings are carried to a considerable depth below the proposed bearing level; building partly on undisturbed soil and partly on uncompacted or poorly compacted fill; unequal loading, where heavily loaded columns settle more than lightly loaded ones; footings bearing at different depths; changes in the groundwater table, including drawdown caused by dewatering on an adjacent site; and organic material such as peat or buried topsoil left in place beneath part of the structure.

Effects. Because the building is forced out of its intended geometry, differential settlement produces cracking in foundation walls, masonry and finishes; floors that slope or become uneven; doors and windows that stick or will not close because their frames are no longer square; distress in the structural frame as members are forced into bending and shear they were not designed for; broken utility connections where pipes enter the building; and, in extreme cases, visible tilting of the structure. The most famous example is the Leaning Tower of Pisa, which tilted because the soil beneath one side of the tower was more compressible than the soil beneath the other.

Prevention. Differential settlement is prevented primarily through adequate subsurface investigation and appropriate foundation design. A thorough site exploration with test pits and borings identifies where the soil varies and whether weak layers lie below the bearing level, which allows the geotechnical engineer to recommend a foundation that will perform consistently. Footings are then proportioned so that the bearing pressure is similar under all of them, rather than making every footing the same size. Where the soil is poor or erratic, a deep foundation can be used to bypass it entirely and carry all of the loads to the same competent stratum or to bedrock, which is the most reliable solution. Other measures include removing unsuitable material and replacing it with engineered compacted fill, founding all footings on undisturbed soil at a consistent depth and below the frost line, using a mat or raft foundation to spread the loads over a single large element that settles as a unit, and tying the structure together with grade beams so that it is stiff enough to resist differential movement. Finally, isolation joints are used where a slab meets walls and columns, so that the slab is free to settle a different amount than the adjacent construction without cracking.

5 · Basic components of a SYMONS formwork system

Check this one against her Lab 6 handout if she has it

SYMONS was covered in Lab 6, which wasn't among the materials I was given. The components below come from Dayton Superior's own Steel-Ply documentation — they manufacture the system — so the names should be standard, but his lab handout is what he'll grade against.

Model answer

The SYMONS Steel-Ply system is a prefabricated, panelized formwork system used primarily for cast-in-place concrete walls. Rather than having carpenters build a form from lumber and plywood on site, the crew assembles a wall form from standardized, reusable components that lock together, which makes erection much faster and allows the forms to be stripped and reused many times. Its basic components are as follows.

Panels. The panel is the form face and the basic building block of the system. It consists of a rugged steel frame — rolled side rails with crossmembers at one-foot centers — holding an inset half-inch High Density Overlay plywood face that is edge sealed against moisture and delamination. The panels weigh roughly five pounds per square foot so they can be handled by hand, the plywood face can be reused up to two hundred times, and the system is offered in over one hundred standard panel and filler sizes.

Wedge bolts. Wedge bolts are the connectors that hold the system together, and they are the feature that makes it fast. They are driven through matching slots in the side rails of adjoining panels and are used in pairs, with one bolt connecting and the other clamping. A single wedge bolt connection joins the panels, any fillers, and the tie all in one operation, so no nails or carpentry are required.

Ties. Ties pass through the wall from one form face to the other. They hold the two faces apart at the correct wall thickness and, more importantly, they carry the outward pressure exerted by the wet concrete — the ties are the components that actually resist the load. The most common type in commercial and industrial work is the S-panel tie, which has a standard one-inch break back, meaning it snaps off one inch inside the concrete face so the steel finishes below the surface and the hole can be patched. The X-flat tie is used on residential foundations where the one-inch break back is not required, and threaded ties, S-base ties, she-bolts and taper ties are available for special conditions.

Walers and waler brackets. Walers are horizontal members, typically two-by-four lumber installed one row per tier of panels, that align the forms and keep the wall straight. They are held in place either by a one-piece waler bracket that inserts into an unused side rail hole or by a Z-tie holder used with a waler tie. It is worth noting that in this system the walers are not structural — their function is alignment, since the ties carry the pressure.

Strongbacks. Strongbacks are vertical alignment members installed at ninety degrees to the walers, commonly at eight feet on center. They are made from doubled two-by-four, two-by-six or two-by-eight lumber and are secured with J-strongback hooks.

Aligners and braces. Turnbuckle form aligners and pipe form aligners, attached with attachment plates and brace kicker brackets and anchored to steel stakes, are used to plumb and position the form. Like the walers, aligners are intended for positioning only and are not designed to brace against or resist concrete pressure.

Fillers and corners. Because walls are rarely an exact multiple of the panel width, panel fillers from four to twenty-two inches in two-inch increments, along with one-inch, one-and-a-half-inch and two-inch steel fillers, are used to make up the odd dimension. All-steel inside corners of four by four or six by six inches and matching outside corners lock adjoining forms together at ninety degrees, and hinged and bay corners are available for other angles.

Scaffold brackets and safety accessories. Scaffold brackets hook onto the panels to support a work platform for the crew, with a maximum safe load of five hundred pounds at a four-to-one safety factor; they must not be used to support cantilevered soffit forms or to store equipment or materials. Safety eyes allow workers to attach safety belts directly to the panels.

In summary, the system consists of steel-framed plywood panels joined by wedge bolts, with ties resisting the concrete pressure and setting the wall thickness, walers and strongbacks aligning the form, turnbuckle aligners plumbing it, and fillers, corners, scaffold brackets and safety accessories completing the assembly.

Must-hit checklist

Three things that will cost points

  • Answering in fragments. The guide says complete sentences. A bullet list of terms, however correct, isn't what was asked for — and on a question like "explain the hydration process," the explanation is the answer.
  • Answering only part of the prompt. "Causes, effects and prevention" is three questions. If you write a brilliant paragraph on causes and stop, you've capped yourself at a third of the marks. Glance back at the prompt before you move on.
  • Leaving out the vocabulary. Saying "the concrete dries and gets hard" when you could say "hydration proceeds as long as moisture is available" is the difference between a C answer and an A answer about the identical idea.
If you run short on time

Write the first sentence of each answer before you fully develop any of them. A partial answer that defines the thing correctly earns far more than a blank, and it protects you if the clock runs out on the last question.
