If you're running behind

If class runs over or the morning slips, cut in this order:

  • Drop the practice test to 10 minutes of true/false only
  • Skim Concrete rather than reading it — the tables carry most of it
  • Read Placing's callout boxes and skip the body text
  • Shorten the break to 10 minutes — but still eat

Never cut: the 11:45 writing block, or the footing problems. Those two are the difference between recognizing the material and being able to produce it.

Three things not to do

  • Don't start anything new after 12:25. New material that late displaces things you already knew and adds nothing but anxiety.
  • Don't re-read as a substitute for recall. Re-reading feels productive and barely works. The drills and the from-memory writing are what move the grade.
  • Don't skip the breaks. Attention falls off a cliff after about 45 minutes. Two ten-minute breaks buy back more than they cost.
Tonight, if it's still tonight

If you're reading this the night before: sleep is not the thing to trade away. Memory consolidation happens overnight, so an extra hour of cramming at 2am costs more in recall than it adds in content. Skim the numbers table, then go to bed and start fresh in the morning.

The one formula

The guide lists "soil bearing capacity and basic footing calculations," so expect arithmetic. Worked examples and unlimited practice on the Foundations page.

Footing Area = Load on Footing ÷ Soil Bearing Capacity
Square footing: Side = √Area
Two traps

Units — if the table is in tsf and the load is in pounds, convert first (1 tsf = 2,000 psf). Don't stop at the area — the question usually wants the side dimension, so take the square root.

Things people mix up

Segregation vs. honeycomb vs. cold joint

DefectWhat it isCaused by
SegregationThe mix separates — aggregate parts from the pasteExcessive vibration · dropping from excessive heights · moving concrete horizontally a long distance
HoneycombVoids in the hardened surface exposing aggregateImproper consolidation
Cold jointA weak seam between liftsDelaying too long between lifts

The four joints

JointPurposeReinforcing across it?
ConstructionDivides work cast on different days; a pour stopYes
ControlGives shrinkage cracking a preferred locationGroove only, ¼ depth
IsolationLets slab and walls/columns settle differentlyNo — slab steel ends
ExpansionGap for thermal expansion; usually not needed indoorsNo

Which part resists the pressure?

In any wall form — site-built or SYMONS — the ties resist the concrete pressure. Walers, strongbacks and aligners only keep the form straight and plumb. Dayton Superior states this explicitly for Steel-Ply: aligners "are not meant to brace or resist concrete pressure."

Hot vs. cold weather curing

Hot — the enemy is evaporation

  • Chilled water or ice in the mix
  • Shade and mist the aggregate stockpiles
  • Use a retarder
  • Pour at a cooler time of day, or at night

Cold — the enemy is freezing

  • Warm (not hot) mixing water
  • Aggregate stored indoors, free of frozen lumps
  • Use Type III cement or an accelerator
  • Never place on frozen ground; curing blankets after

On exam day

  • Budget time for the written responses first. They're worth the most and they take the longest. Don't let multiple choice eat the clock.
  • Answer every part of a written prompt. "Causes, effects and prevention" is three questions in one.
  • Complete sentences — the guide says so explicitly. Bullet fragments lose points even when the content is right.
  • Watch absolutes on true/false. "Expansion joints are always required" is false — usually not required indoors.
  • If a question asks "why," the answer usually involves water — too much weakens concrete, too little stops hydration, losing it too fast cracks it.
