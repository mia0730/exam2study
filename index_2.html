The one formula

There's a calculation on this exam, and it's a short one. Full worked examples and unlimited practice on the Foundations page.

Footing Area = Load on Footing ÷ Soil Bearing Capacity
Square footing: Side = √Area
Two traps

Units — if the table is in tsf and the load is in pounds, convert first (1 tsf = 2,000 psf). Don't stop at the area — the question usually wants the side dimension, so take the square root.

Four things people mix up

If you only have ten minutes, spend them here.

1. Segregation vs. honeycomb vs. a cold joint

DefectWhat it isCaused by
SegregationThe mix separates — aggregate parts from the pasteExcessive vibration, dropping from excessive heights, moving concrete horizontally a long distance
HoneycombVoids in the hardened surface where you can see the aggregateImproper consolidation — the concrete never filled the form
Cold jointA visible seam where one lift hardened before the next was placedDelaying too long between lifts

2. The four joints

JointPurposeDoes reinforcing cross it?
ConstructionDivides work cast on different days; acts as a pour stopYes — runs across to lock the parts together
ControlGives shrinkage cracking a preferred locationGroove only, cut ¼ depth
IsolationSeparates slab from walls/columns so they can settle differentlyNo — slab reinforcing ends at the joint
ExpansionA gap allowing concrete to expand in heat; usually not needed indoorsNo — reinforcing stops each side

3. Screed → bull float → float → trowel

All four smooth the slab, but at different moments and for different reasons. The full timeline is on the Placing & Curing page — the short version:

  • Screed — levels it to the top of the forms. Immediately. Leaves ridges.
  • Bull float — right after screeding. Brings up the paste, fills voids.
  • Float — only after bleed water has evaporated. Drives aggregate down.
  • Trowel — last. Produces the smooth, dense surface. (Or a broom instead, for skid resistance.)

4. Hot weather vs. cold weather curing

Hot — the enemy is evaporation

  • Chilled water or ice in the mix
  • Shade and mist the aggregate stockpiles
  • Use a retarder to slow hydration
  • Pour at a cooler time of day, or at night

Cold — the enemy is freezing

  • Warm (not hot) mixing water
  • Store aggregate indoors, free of frozen lumps and ice
  • Use Type III cement or an accelerator
  • Never place on frozen ground; cover with curing blankets

On exam day

  • Sequencing questions are the most likely format for this material, since nearly everything in Weeks 6–7 is a process. If you're asked what comes next, picture the slab actually being built.
  • Watch for absolutes on true/false. "Expansion joints are always required" is false — they're usually not required indoors.
  • Do the matching fast and bank the time for anything that needs thinking.
  • If a question asks "why," the answer is almost always about water: too much water weakens concrete, too little stops hydration, and losing water too fast cracks it.
