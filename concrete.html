2.1 · Concrete characteristics

What makes it great

  • Made from readily available materials; used throughout the world
  • Fluid when fresh — can be formed into any shape
  • Durable (but porous)
  • Does not rot
  • Fire resistant
  • Does not rust — though the rebar embedded in it may
  • Cost effective & durable for foundations — footings, pile caps, grade beams, basement slabs & walls
  • Competitive with steel and masonry for structural frames — girders, beams, columns, bearing walls

What limits it

  • Strong in compression but weak in tension — needs reinforcing to carry tension
  • May degrade with exposure to severe conditions — freeze-thaw, harsh chemicals
  • Quality & strength depend entirely on quality of ingredients, proper blending, and good handling & curing practices

That last bullet is the thesis of the entire rest of this course. Concrete is only as good as how you handle it — which is why Weeks 6 and 7 exist.

2.2 · What is concrete?

A rocklike material composed of:

Portland cement

The glue.

Aggregate

The rock and sand — 60–80% of the volume.

Clean water

What makes the cement react.

Optional admixtures

Liquids or powders, added during mixing, which alter the qualities of fresh or hardened concrete.

Worth noting for the augered-pile question on the Foundations page: grout is the same thing but with no gravel.

2.3 · Portland cement

What it isA man-made product — a fine powder
Patented byJoseph Aspdin, an English mason, in 1824
Why "Portland"A generic term — named for the Isle of Portland off the coast of England. It is not a brand
Sold in94 lb bags (sacks)
What it doesReacts with water to form a gel that hardens and binds the other ingredients together. It is the glue in concrete
The reactionCalled hydration — and it is exothermic (it gives off heat)
ClinkerThe intermediate product of the cement manufacturing process — lumps out of the rotary kiln that are then ground with gypsum into finished cement
Hold onto "exothermic"

Hydration gives off heat. That one fact explains Type IV cement, retarders, mass-concrete problems, and why curing blankets work in cold weather by trapping the heat of hydration. It shows up again on the curing section.

The five cement types

TypeNameUsed for
Type INormalMost applications
Type IIModerate Sulfate ResistantModerate levels of salt
Type IIIHigh Early StrengthFaster strength gain — cold weather work
Type IVLow Heat of HydrationTo reduce heat buildup — massive pours like dams
Type VSulfate ResistantHigher levels of salt

White Portland is a sixth worth knowing: properties similar to Type I except color. Provides a lighter, more consistent color for architectural concrete, terrazzo flooring and stucco.

How to remember I–V

I = ordinary. II and V are the salt pair (II moderate, V high). III = fast (think "III in a hurry"). IV = cool and slow. The two that come up most on this exam are III (cold weather) and IV (heat control).

2.4 · Aggregates

60–80% of the volume of concrete. Aggregates must be:

  • Clean, Strong
  • Resistant to freeze-thaw
  • Chemically stable
  • Properly graded (a good spread of particle sizes so they pack together)

ASTM laboratory tests are used to determine suitability.

Types of aggregate

  • Normal weight materials — crushed stone & sand from the quarry; bank run sand & gravel
  • Lightweight processed materials — expanded shale

The two types of concrete

Normal Weight Structural Concrete

  • 140–155 pounds per cubic foot when hardened
  • Made with aggregate from the bank or quarries — commonly occurring rock or stone
  • Usually screened to get proper sizes and washed to remove fines (dust & silt/clay particles)

Lightweight Structural Concrete

  • 95–120 pounds per cubic foot
  • Expanded shale is often used. Shale is a rock formed in nature from hardened deposits of clay
  • It is heated in a kiln to soften it; water in the shale causes it to expand like popcorn, making it porous & lighter
  • More expensive than normal weight (cost to process)
Lightweight concreteDetail
AdvantageReduced dead load means smaller supporting members — columns, foundation
DisadvantagesNot as strong as normal weight concrete. In high rise frames, lightweight is OK for floors and beams, but not used in columns or foundations

2.5 · The four common admixtures

Learn these as two pairs: two that change the timing, two that change the quality.

Accelerator speeds up

  • Speeds up the set of concrete — helps concrete gain strength faster
  • Useful when concrete is placed in cold weather — reduces the critical curing time when heat may have to be provided
  • Used in precast work to speed up production
  • Used to increase production in cast-in-place concrete frames — each floor can be completed faster

Retarder slows down

  • Slows down the set of concrete
  • Reduces heat of hydration in hot weather concrete work — less shrinkage cracking
  • Allows more time for crews to finish concrete in hot weather

Air Entrainer durability

  • Induces microscopic air bubbles in hardened concrete
  • Increases durability of concrete to freeze/thaw conditions
  • Extremely important in slabs, sidewalks and bridge decks exposed to weather

Why it works: water expands when it freezes. Those tiny bubbles give the expanding ice somewhere to go instead of fracturing the concrete.

Superplasticizer workability

  • Increases the workability (slump) of concrete without adding more water
  • Important to have high slumps for better workability when concrete is to be pumped, or when lots of closely spaced rebar makes consolidation difficult
  • Can also be used to reduce the water in a batch — thus reducing the water/cement ratio for stronger concrete without sacrificing workability, so the batch isn't too stiff
The water/cement idea — this is the big one

More water makes concrete easier to place but weaker. Less water makes it stronger but harder to work with. That tension is why a superplasticizer exists: it buys you workability without the water. Lower water/cement ratio = stronger concrete.

Slump is the measure of workability — how far a cone of fresh concrete sags when the mold is lifted off it, measured in inches in the slump test. High slump = wet and flowable. Low slump = stiff.

Classic exam pairing

Which admixture for cold weather? Accelerator (and Type III cement). Which for hot weather? Retarder. Don't flip them — and notice both answers also appear in the curing section.

2.6 · Concrete reinforcing

The theory of location — memorize this phrasing

Concrete has no useful tensile strength. Reinforcing carries tension. So:

"Place reinforcing where the concrete is in tension."

Most reinforcing is made of steel, in the form of:

  • Deformed bars — the ridges ("deformations") let the bar grip the concrete
  • Welded wire fabric (WWF)
  • Pre-stressing & post-tensioning cables (tendons)

2.7 · Patterns of reinforcing

For each member, ask the same question: where is this thing in tension? The bars go there.

Footings

  • Longitudinal & transverse bars near the bottom carry tension due to upward soil pressure. (The soil pushes up in the middle while the column pushes down — the footing bends, and the bottom goes into tension.)
  • Dowels are needed to tie the vertical bars in the wall to the footing.

Walls

  • Rows of vertical and horizontal bars are usually placed on both faces of the wall
  • Lap splices are used to tie vertical bars to dowels — bars overlap so the load transfers between them
  • Cover refers to the minimum amount of concrete that must surround the bars closest to outer surfaces. Cover protects the steel from corrosion and fire

Columns

ElementWhat it does
Vertical (longitudinal) barsArranged in a square or circular pattern. Carry compression & sometimes tension caused by lateral loads on the frame. Steel is stronger than concrete, so using bars in compression reduces the cross sectional area of a column
Ties or spiralsSmall bars wrapped around the outside of the vertical bars. Usually No. 3 or 4 bars. Spirals are continuous; ties are spaced at regular intervals. They help prevent the longitudinal bars from buckling

Tie arrangement may vary depending on the magnitude of loads on the column and the o.c. spacing of the vertical bars. Column reinforcing can be prefabricated and lifted into position by crane, with the rebar offset at the top of the column for a lap splice to the next lift.

Beams

Beams under load are subjected to bending & shearing that cause tension and potential cracking:

  • Longitudinal bars are used at the top and bottom of the beam — bottom bars at midspan where the beam sags, top bars over the columns where it hogs
  • Stirrups — light U-shaped bars — are placed at close spacing and wrap around the main longitudinal bars
  • Stirrups are designed to cross any potential diagonal cracks — that's what they're for: shear
  • Horizontal bars in the beam are bent and lapped at the ends for better anchorage
Ties vs. stirrups — easy to confuse

Both are small bars wrapped around bigger bars. Ties (or spirals) are in columns and stop the vertical bars buckling. Stirrups are in beams and cross diagonal shear cracks. Column = tie. Beam = stirrup.

2.8 · Formwork

Formwork is the responsibility of the contractor — but the contractor may be required to submit shop drawings for formwork to the architect/engineer for approval.

Three formwork options

Build forms on siteUsing framing lumber & plywood
Rent or buy prefabricated (panelized) formsReusable manufactured panels
Have forms custom builtA very expensive alternative, but may be the only option to form irregular surfaces

Components of contractor-built wall forms

Three groups. This is prime matching-question material.

Form support system

  • Studs run vertically between top & bottom plates
  • Wales run horizontally to support the studs & keep the forms straight
  • Braces position & align the forms

Form "facing" material

  • Plywood or boards, attached to studs & plates
  • Comes in contact with the concrete & imparts the surface appearance
  • A form liner can be used inside the form to give concrete a special texture or pattern

Spreaders & form ties

  • Hold the two sides of the form together against the pressure of the wet concrete
  • Types include the snap tie and the screw tie. Snap ties have breaking points so the ends snap off after stripping

Form liners

  • Made from fiberglass, plastic or rubber
  • Used for architectural concrete
  • Examples: a fluted surface pattern, geometric shapes, or imitation stone

Two manufactured systems to know by name

  • Panelized systems — prefabricated modular panels (covered further in Lab 6)
  • Flying forms — flying table forms for slabs, lifted by crane from one completed floor straight up to the next, saving the time of dismantling and rebuilding
Wale, again

Wale appeared on Exam 1 as the horizontal beam on excavation sheeting. Here it's the horizontal member on a wall form. Same idea both times: a horizontal member that backs up a vertical surface and spreads out the pressure.

2.9 · Lab 3 — why steel? Lab 3

Lab 3 was Tying in Reinforcing for Concrete, and its post-lab was a bending test comparing metal wires — steel, quenched steel, aired (annealed) steel, copper and aluminum — hanging masses from each and measuring the net displacement.

What the results showed

  • Steel had the smallest net displacement for a given mass — it is the stiffest and strongest of the metals tested, deflecting the least under the same load.
  • Aluminum showed the most permanent (plastic) deformation — it bent and stayed bent.
  • Heat treatment changes behavior: quenching (rapid cooling) makes steel harder and stiffer; annealing/airing (slow cooling) makes it softer and more ductile.
The point of the lab

This is why rebar is steel. Reinforcing has to carry tension without stretching much, because if the steel stretches, the concrete around it cracks. Steel's combination of high tensile strength, high stiffness, and a thermal expansion rate close to concrete's is what makes it the right material — and no other common metal comes close.
