6.1 · Purpose and function of formwork

Formwork is the temporary structure that holds fresh concrete in the desired shape and position until it has hardened enough to support itself.

It does four jobs at once:

  • Shapes the concrete — concrete is fluid when fresh and can be formed into any shape, so the form is what determines the finished geometry
  • Supports the load — it carries the weight of the wet concrete, the reinforcing steel, the workers and their equipment
  • Resists the pressure — fresh concrete behaves like a liquid and pushes outward on the form faces
  • Gives the surface its appearance — the facing material comes in contact with the concrete and imparts the surface appearance
Responsibility — a likely question

Formwork is the responsibility of the contractor. But the contractor may be required to submit shop drawings for the formwork to the architect/engineer for approval — and for an elevated slab, engineered shop drawings must be prepared, submitted and approved before formwork erection begins.

Why formwork is such a big deal on a concrete job: it is typically the largest single cost in cast-in-place concrete work — often more than the concrete and the steel combined — because it is labor intensive and has to be built, braced, stripped and often rebuilt on every floor. That's the reason panelized systems, flying forms and re-shoring all exist: they're all ways of reusing formwork faster.

6.2 · Common formwork materials and systems

The three options the contractor chooses between

OptionWhat it meansWhen you'd use it
Build forms on siteUsing framing lumber & plywoodSmall jobs, odd one-off shapes, low reuse
Rent or buy prefabricated (panelized) formsManufactured modular panels — SYMONS is the classic exampleRepetitive work, many reuses, faster cycles
Have forms custom builtPurpose-made for this projectA very expensive alternative, but may be the only option to form irregular surfaces

Materials

  • Plywood — the usual facing material. Panelized systems use High Density Overlay (HDO) plywood, which is edge-sealed against moisture and delamination so it survives many reuses
  • Dimension lumber — studs, wales, strongbacks, braces, blocking
  • Steel — panel frames, ties, hardware, and column forms
  • Aluminum — beams in falsework systems; lighter to handle
  • Cardboard / fiber tubes — Sonotubes for round columns, which leave a spiral marking on the stripped concrete
  • Fiberglass, plastic, rubber — form liners, used for architectural concrete to give a fluted pattern, geometric shapes or imitation stone

Named systems to know

  • Panelized systems — prefabricated modular panels (SYMONS below)
  • Flying forms — flying table forms for slabs, lifted by crane from a completed floor straight up to the next, avoiding the time of dismantling and rebuilding
  • Falsework — the scaffolding tower and beam system that supports elevated slab formwork
  • Pans — reusable forms that create ribs or waffles in the underside of a slab

6.3 · Components of contractor-built wall forms

Three groups. Learn them as groups and the SYMONS components map onto the same logic.

1 · Form support system

  • Studs run vertically, between the top & bottom plates
  • Wales run horizontally to support the studs & keep the forms straight
  • Braces position & align the forms

2 · Form "facing" material

  • Plywood or boards, attached to the studs & plates
  • Comes in contact with the concrete & imparts the surface appearance
  • A form liner can be used inside the form to give the concrete a special texture or pattern

3 · Spreaders & form ties

  • Hold the two sides of the form together against the pressure of the wet concrete, and hold them apart at the correct wall thickness
  • Snap ties have breaking points so the projecting ends snap off after stripping; screw ties are the other common type
The distinction that matters most

Ties resist the concrete pressure. Wales and braces only align. If you remember nothing else about form hardware, remember which part is actually holding the wall together — it's the ties.

6.4 · The SYMONS forming system Written response ★

Where this came from

SYMONS was covered in Lab 6, and that handout wasn't among the materials I was given — the Week 5 slide just says "Panelized Systems: Discussed in LAB 6." So the component names below come from Dayton Superior's own Steel-Ply documentation (they make it), not from your professor's slides. The system is standardized, so the names should match — but if she has the Lab 6 handout, check the component names against it, because that's what he'll grade from.

SYMONS Steel-Ply is the most widely used panelized (prefabricated) wall forming system in North America. It's a kit of modular steel-framed panels that lock together with wedge bolts, so a crew can build a wall form out of standard parts instead of carpentering one from scratch — and then strip it and reuse it on the next pour.

The components

Steel-Ply panels — the form face

A rugged steel frame — rolled side rails with crossmembers at one-foot centers — with an inset ½" High Density Overlay (HDO) plywood face that is edge sealed against moisture and delamination.

  • About 5 lbs per square foot, so panels are light enough to handle by hand
  • The plywood face can be reused up to 200 times
  • Over 100 standard panel and filler sizes

Wedge bolts — the connector

The signature feature of the system. Wedge bolts are driven through the slots in the side rails of adjoining panels. They are used in pairs — one connects, one clamps.

A wedge bolt joins the panels, the fillers AND the tie in one operation, which is exactly why the system is fast. No nails, no carpentry.

Ties — what actually resists the pressure

Ties pass through the wall between the two form faces. They hold the faces at the correct wall thickness and carry the outward pressure of the wet concrete.

  • S-Panel tie — the most common for commercial and industrial work; standard 1" break back
  • X-Flat tie — used for residential foundations where the 1" break back isn't required
  • Others: threaded tie (battered walls), S-base tie (bottom rail / against existing walls), she-bolts and taper ties (reusable, for wider tie spacing)

Break back is how far inside the concrete face the tie snaps off when it's broken after stripping — so the steel ends up below the surface and the hole can be patched.

Walers — alignment, not strength

Usually 2x4 lumber, one row per tier of panels, running horizontally along the form. They align the forms and keep the wall straight.

Walers in this system are not structural — the ties carry the pressure. Held in place by a one-piece waler bracket (inserted into an unused side rail hole, with a wedge) or a Z-tie holder with a waler tie.

Strongbacks — vertical stiffeners

Vertical alignment members placed at 90° to the walers, commonly at 8 ft on center. Doubled 2x4, 2x6 or 2x8 lumber, secured with J-strongback hooks.

Aligners / braces — plumbing the wall

Turnbuckle form aligners (adjustable, anchored to a steel stake) and pipe form aligners, attached with attachment plates and brace kicker brackets.

Important: aligners are for positioning the forms — they are NOT meant to brace against or resist concrete pressure. Same principle as the walers.

Fillers & corners — making up odd dimensions

  • Panel fillers — 4" to 22" wide in 2" increments (plus a 5"), used to close gaps between panels when the wall length isn't a round number
  • Steel fillers — 1", 1½" and 2", for fine adjustment
  • Inside corners (4"×4" or 6"×6") and outside corners — all steel, locking adjoining forms at 90°
  • Hinged corners and bay corners for angles other than 90°

Scaffold brackets & safety

Scaffold brackets hook onto the panels to support a work platform. Maximum safe load 500 lbs (4:1 safety factor), and they must not be used to support cantilevered soffit forms or to store equipment or materials.

Safety eyes let workers clip safety belts directly to the panels.

If you remember six things about SYMONS

Panels (steel frame + HDO plywood face) · Wedge bolts (connect panels, fillers and ties in one operation) · Ties (set wall thickness and resist the pressure) · Walers (align only, not structural) · Strongbacks (vertical, 90° to walers) · Aligners/braces (plumb the wall, don't resist pressure). Then mention fillers, corners and scaffold brackets to round it out.

Drill: order the SYMONS components the way you'd assemble and describe them

How a SYMONS wall goes together

The same sequence as any wall form, done with parts instead of lumber:

  1. Set the first side of panels on the footing, connecting panel to panel with wedge bolts
  2. Insert the ties through the side rail slots as you go, locked by the same wedge bolts
  3. Add fillers and corners to make up the exact wall length and turn the corners
  4. Install walers in their brackets and strongbacks across them
  5. Erect the second side over the projecting tie ends and wedge-bolt it
  6. Plumb and align with turnbuckle aligners; add scaffold brackets where crews need to work
  7. Place concrete in lifts; strip after it sets; break the ties back and point & patch the holes

6.5 · Installation, bracing and removal

Installation — the wall sequence

From the Week 7 lecture, and worth knowing in order:

Install forms on ONE side, including ties

Then anchor & brace, and coat with form oil so the concrete won't bond to the form.

Install reinforcing

Install embeds & cutouts, then bulkheads (end stops)

Inspect

The last chance to see inside before the wall is closed up.

Erect the second side, plumb & brace, establish pour height

Place in lifts, using a drop tube; consolidate with vibration

Finish, cure

Strip forms, break ties, point & patch

Form pressure — why bracing matters

Faster pour rate= higher stresses on the formwork
Pressure against the formsincreases with height — the deeper the liquid head, the harder it pushes
As concrete setspressures are reduced — it stops behaving like a liquid

So tall walls are poured in lifts, to take advantage of set, minimize form design loads, and minimize buckling or blowouts. But you cannot delay too long between lifts or you get an unwanted cold joint — the first lift hardens before the second is placed, leaving a weak seam with no bond across it.

Removal (stripping)

  • Vertical forms (walls, columns) carry no permanent load, so they can come off relatively early — once the concrete can hold its own shape and surface
  • Elevated slab forms carry the slab itself, so they stay until the concrete can support itself — often 75% of design strength, which may take 7 to 14 days depending on temperatures and the type of cement used
  • After stripping a wall: break the ties and point & patch the tie holes and any surface defects

6.6 · Shoring and re-shoring

Shoring

The original temporary support holding up the formwork and the fresh slab while it is placed and cures. In an elevated floor system this is the falsework — the scaffolding towers and beams carrying the decking.

Re-shoring

Temporary posts set back under a completed floor after the original formwork has been stripped, while the concrete continues to gain strength.

The three facts about re-shoring
  • Done to support construction loads on a completed floor while the concrete continues to gain strength
  • Construction loads include the weight of the formwork for the next level
  • Re-shoring allows for a faster cycle time to complete each floor

The logic, in one sentence: a floor at 75% strength is strong enough to strip but not strong enough to carry the weight of building the next floor on top of it — so re-shores go back under it and you keep moving up instead of waiting 28 days per floor.

6.7 · Formwork safety and common causes of failure

Formwork failure is one of the most dangerous events on a concrete job, because it happens suddenly, while the concrete is liquid and crews are working on and around the form.

The two failures named in the lecture

Buckled form

The form bends out of shape under the pressure, leaving a bulged, out-of-tolerance wall.

Blowout

Formwork failure in which the concrete bursts out of the form entirely — the form comes apart under pressure.

Common causes

CauseWhat goes wrong
Placing too fastA faster pour rate means higher pressure than the form was designed for. The single most common cause
Pouring too high in one liftPressure increases with height; exceeding the designed pour height overloads the ties and panels
Inadequate or missing tiesThe ties are what resist the pressure. Too few, wrong type, or ones left out create a weak spot that blows out
Inadequate bracing or shoringForms move, go out of plumb, or the falsework fails under the load
Improper stripping — too earlyRemoving slab forms before the concrete has gained enough strength can collapse the floor
Failure to follow the shop drawingsThe formwork was engineered for a specific pour rate, height and tie layout; deviating from it voids the design
Damaged or over-reused componentsBent panels, worn ties, delaminated plywood faces
Unbalanced or eccentric loadingDumping concrete on one side, or piling material on a scaffold bracket beyond its 500 lb limit
Vibration too close to the form faceOver-vibrating against the form increases local pressure and can also cause segregation

Safety practices

  • Build to the approved engineered shop drawings, and don't exceed the design pour rate or pour height
  • Inspect the formwork before the pour — and keep watching it during the pour, so a problem is caught before it becomes a blowout
  • Use scaffold brackets for work platforms, within the 500 lb limit; use safety eyes for fall protection tie-off
  • Keep crews clear of the area where a blowout would discharge
  • Don't strip until the concrete has reached the specified strength; set re-shores where required

Tie-back to Exam 1: fall protection is OSHA's most frequently cited violation, and falls are the leading cause of death among construction workers — which is why the scaffold bracket and the safety eye exist on this system at all.
